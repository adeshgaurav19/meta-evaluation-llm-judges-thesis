\chapter{Conclusion}

At the beginning of this thesis, we had two questions to consider. ``Can we rely on LLM judges for evaluating RAG systems with poisoned data, and how susceptible are these judges to the severity of poisoned data?'' and ``Can a pre-filter be devised to enable the LLM judges to mitigate this vulnerability?''

Experimental results answered the two questions with clarity. They demonstrated that the vulnerability is real and significant. The baseline faithfulness false positive rates for three judges range from 0.412 to 0.699. The results show that the three judges have different degrees of sensitivity towards different attack classes. The reason is that judges have different scoring regimes and calibration. The most treacherous class, PoisonedRAG-style, further made this pattern clear. Judges with relatively continuous scoring and lenient behaviour found it hardest to distinguish the attacks and punish them, while for the judge with a near-binary scoring pattern, it was easier to identify and punish them. This suggests that vulnerability is not evenly distributed across the entire LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm but depends on the design of judges and types of attack.

The second finding was more unexpected. The two pre-filtering approaches evaluated in this thesis did not reduce judge vulnerability consistently. After separating the poisoned triplets into True and Survived subsets, based on whether the supporting passages remained untouched, an unusual pattern came into the picture. In most of the cases, the faithfulness scores actually increased instead of going down.
This behaviour appeared across all three judges after statistical filtering, although the effect was stronger for some models than others. DeepSeek showed the clearest increase, while GPT and Gemma changed more moderately. Under the clustered bootstrap evaluation, only the DeepSeek True-subset effect approached statistical significance.

The biggest impact seems to be found in the Survived subset with PoisonedRAG-style attacks. In those situations, DeepSeek, by and large, produced higher faithfulness scores once the context had been filtered. This might be because, although the poisoned content is still there, the shorter filtered context seemed ``cleaner'' and more trustworthy to the judge. We saw only a minor impact on answer relevance during the different experimental phases, which is expected, as we left the answers completely untouched.

The primary reason for this behaviour seems to be the removal of distractors. The statistical filter is intended to remove poisoned passages, but in the process, it removed a significant amount of the original HotpotQA distractors as well. The residual context was shorter (average 7.1 passages versus 11.6, 39\% decrease) and more focused on the topic. Judges are giving higher faithfulness scores to this cleaner remainder regardless of whether the supporting evidence is still present or not. We directly tested the opposite collateral damage hypothesis (that the filter removed the supporting passages and judges lost the contradictory evidence), but this was not supported by the experiments. In triplets where the filter removed supporting passages, we saw a drop in scores for all three judges by 0.15 to 0.23; judges are clearly scoring the missing supporting passages as important. The paradox arises only when the filter leaves the supporting passage alone, and is more prominent when the filter removes most of the original distractors. This was unexpected but informative.

Combined, these two results (Judges can be fragile and filtering surprisingly damages the worst-case) imply an obvious practical consequence: pre-filtering cannot be argued as a way to mitigate the harms caused by using an LLM-as-a-Judge when the underlying system is poisoned. Anyone deploying an RAG evaluation pipeline and considering security-critical applications should not count on additional upstream filtering for increased downstream reliability. Based on the empirical evidence, adding upstream filtering can make things worse.


\section{Restating the Contributions}

In summary, this thesis has four key contributions.

The first contribution is a Poisoned Evaluation Dataset (v2) composed of 2,400 question-context-answer triplets extracted from HotpotQA with different poisoning methods and at varying degrees of corruption. This dataset was specifically created to examine LLM judge behaviour in the presence of an adversarially manipulated evaluation corpus. We also provide the poisoning strategy to easily reproduce the same effect on other datasets.

The second contribution is a multi-metric meta-evaluation framework to examine judge robustness to corpus poisoning. The proposed framework is used to evaluate 3 modern LLM judges on conventional RAGAS metrics while also evaluating their calibration behaviour, the distributions of their scores, and their susceptibility to attack-specific behaviours.

The third contribution is a lightweight, statistically-based pre-filter composed of several engineered features combined via an XGBoost classifier. This filter is evaluated alongside another LLM-based filter with the aim to study individual filtering performance and impact on downstream judges.

The final contribution is an end-to-end, residual-aware evaluation framework, inspired by the True/Survived decomposition, which aims to determine whether or not a filter reduces the impact of a poison attack, as opposed to simply reducing the perceived faithfulness of the evaluation by eliminating distracting material.


\section{Answers to the Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1: How badly are contemporary LLM judges fooled by corpus poisoning?}

As can be observed from the experiments, current LLM judges are highly susceptible to poisoned retrieval contexts. There is variation depending on model and attack: The baseline false positive rates on faithfulness vary from 0.412 for DeepSeek to 0.699 for Gemma. These variations hold similarly on metrics of context relevance and answer relevance. Also, as we vary the attacks, they have a differing effect on the judges; in particular PoisonedRAG-style attacks seem particularly difficult for GPT and Gemma, though less so for DeepSeek.

\textbf{RQ2: How does that vulnerability vary with judge calibration?}

The three judges have rather distinguishable calibration behaviours under adversarial attacks. DeepSeek acts more cautiously and resembles more of a near-binary yes-or-no detector whereas GPT has a more continuous scoring pattern. However, Gemma appears to be more susceptible in several attack settings due to more default-faithful bias or simply being more lenient. These variations may explain why judge calibration and scoring behaviour matter.

\textbf{RQ3: Can a lightweight upstream pre-filter mitigate the failure?}

The data appears to suggest that using lightweight filtering is not a sufficient defence. The statistical pre-filter is an effective standalone classifier, but its effect on judge robustness is more erratic. Several cases actually show increased faithfulness on the poisoned contexts when filtering is used. We note the most visible effects are with DeepSeek on PoisonedRAG-style attacks, where the filtered contexts can actually seem more credible to the judge while still being adversarial. The LLM filter tells the same story, just quieter. It removes fewer passages on average (1.2 versus 4.5 for the statistical filter), but it still nudges faithfulness upward on poisoned contexts. A weaker version of the same problem.

\textbf{RQ4: What mechanisms explain the failure of pre-filtering to mitigate?}

Our interpretation is that the crucial factor isn't the poison's removal per se, but the alterations in the residual context that result from filtering. The filter removes the distractor texts in most cases, creating cleaner, shorter residual contexts, which judges perceive as closer to a trustworthy retrieved context. Moreover, the findings do not support the alternative explanation that the paradox stems largely from removing evidence supporting the poison. Rather, the filter appears to enhance judges' confidence without explicit reason in cases where the structure of the residual context becomes simpler. The LLM filter confirms this reading. It removes fewer passages and produces a milder version of the same effect --- a slightly cleaner, slightly shorter residual that judges still find more credible than the unfiltered one.


\section{Future Work}

Four directions come out of this work as the most natural next steps.

\textbf{Cross-task generalisation.} Everything in this study was done on HotpotQA. That is a multi-hop QA benchmark where questions need two supporting passages to answer, and distractor passages, often totally irrelevant, are built into the context. The attack design and the filter design both are based on that structure. Whether the paradox remains valid with respect to single-hop QA, dialogue evaluation, or summarisation we are yet to confirm. We suspect that the mechanism for removing distractors will carry over to any retrieval task which interleaves supporting passages with loosely related noise, but that in high-precision retrieval tasks with little or no distractors it may have no impact at all because the filter has nothing `off-topic' to remove by mistake. The truth of this will require testing on at least one other task of significantly different structure.

\textbf{Judge-side hardening.} The justification analysis discussed in Section~4.6 was done on a small sample, only 28 triplets and one judge. We decided to keep it small on purpose as we were constrained by budget. More output tokens across 2{,}400 triplets would have increased the cost many times over. But the directional signal is large enough to take seriously. When GPT was given an explicit poison-aware prompt, mean faithfulness on PoisonedRAG-style triplets dropped from 0.800 to 0.492. On adversarial fact it dropped from 0.758 to 0.558. That is a meaningful shift from a simple prompt change, no new filter required. We think this is the most promising direction the data actually points toward, with the caveat that 28 triplets is not enough to draw firm conclusions. Testing this at scale across all three judges and all attack types is a direct next step.

\textbf{Hybrid filter architectures matched to the True/Survived decomposition.} The statistical filter and the LLM filter catch different things. The statistical filter is stronger on random noise and adversarial fact. Mistral is stronger on PoisonedRAG-style. A hybrid that combines both would in principle get higher recall. But higher recall may not help if the distractor-removal mechanism persists, because the filter would remove even more of the original context, producing an even cleaner-looking residual that judges over-trust. The more useful question is whether a filter can be designed that removes poison without also removing original distractors. Re-ranking, contradiction flagging, or filters trained to distinguish adversarial poison from natural retrieval noise are worth exploring.

\textbf{Confidence abstention and downstream pipeline design.} GPT produces continuous scores spread across the full range. That creates a natural band where the judge is genuinely uncertain. A pipeline that abstains on triplets with faithfulness scores between 0.4 and 0.7 and routes those to human review could, based on the score distributions in Section~4.1.2, catch a meaningful share of remaining errors with a 10 to 15\% abstention rate. We did not implement this, but the cost-benefit question is straightforward to answer with the existing data.
The bigger problem is the same across all four directions. Evaluation infrastructure is becoming a target in its own right. This thesis measured one piece of that on one benchmark. The rest is still open.


\section{Closing Remarks}

We are releasing the Poisoned Evaluation Dataset and Methodology, the pre-filter pipeline implementation, and the complete experimental codebase with this thesis. This work was completed with limited resources and we hope these artefacts will serve future researchers without them having to reproduce our experiments entirely.

Our main empirical finding is worrying. The problem is not only that LLM judges have limitations; a corpus poisoning attack succeeds precisely by targeting those limitations. The further problem is that a filter, properly implemented to remove the poison, does not work as expected under our tested configurations. It fails to re-introduce correctness and in cases where judge performance is critical, may in fact diminish it. Our contribution is not only an experimental measurement that substantiates these claims across 3 contemporary LLM judges, 3 attack types, 4 noise levels, on a single multi-hop QA task, but a formal framework with the True/Survived decomposition---to identify exactly where the filter is performing and failing. Although this thesis only covers a single multi-hop QA task, the main paradox found herein should be taken as a proven phenomenon only for the domain of HotpotQA-like multi-hop retrieval QA. It has not been generalised across other tasks yet, but further experimental investigation along this avenue is an immediate next step.

In short, this work suggests an argument for an aggressive, rather than complacent approach to the deployment of LLM-as-a-Judge models in hostile environments. A judge model is not simply a passive component in a downstream application, whose integrity can be taken for granted after upstream mitigation. A judge model can be considered another component which is vulnerable. Our main goal is that by measuring the extent of the problem, as well as the shortcomings of proposed solutions on a common set of benchmarks, this work will help encourage further efforts in developing automatic evaluation systems whose integrity cannot be questioned, when integrity really counts.
