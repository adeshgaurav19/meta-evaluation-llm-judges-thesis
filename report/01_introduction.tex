\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}

It won’t be wrong to state that we have seen an extraordinary transformation in the field of artificial intelligence driven by the introduction and adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) over the last three years. Models from OpenAI, Gemini, Anthropic and other players have shown commendable capabilities in natural language understanding, generation and reasoning. These capabilities have made organisations shift how they approach knowledge-intensive tasks to language-driven architectures.
One of the most important architectural developments from this era is that of a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). It was first formalised by \citet{lewis2020retrieval} to address the key limitations of standalone LLMs: Hallucinations and the inability to access knowledge post their training data. By including a retriever with a generative model, RAG systems ground their output in external knowledge databases. This enables them to have access to and control of updated domain-specific information and has become the standard for enterprise-grade AI applications in sectors like Finance, Legal and Healthcare.
The expansion of RAG systems introduced a critical evaluation bottleneck. How do organisations verify that their RAG systems are working as expected, providing accurate relevant and grounded outputs. Traditional evaluation methods included a human evaluating and annotating or reference based scores like BLEU or ROUGE. Both of these methods are either too expensive or fail to capture the nuances of generative outputs. This gap gave birth to the LLM-as-a-Judge concept, in which a LLM is tasked to evaluate the outputs of another model by giving scores on basis of different criteria. Frameworks like RAGAS \citep{es2024ragas}, G-Eval \citep{liu2023geval} and others are now deploying LLM judges for scaled and automated evaluation.
The appeal of this concept is clear: LLM Judges offer scalability, consistency and alignment with human evaluators at a fraction of the cost. However, this adoption relies on an assumption that the context or the information provided to the judges is trustworthy. In reality, RAG systems are vulnerable to corpus poisoning attacks, in which meticulously crafted adversarial passages and chunks of data are injected to point the system to wrong answers. When a judge sees a wrong response and finds a relevant poisoned passage, it may assign high faithfulness and other scores that are consistent technically even though factually wrong.
Recent research has begun to expose the fragility of this assumption. Studies by \citet{raina2024llm} showcased that short adversarial phrases can systematically fool LLM Judges into assigning higher scores, regardless of the actual quality of the text. \citet{zou2025poisonedrag} introduced the PoisonedRAG framework, highlighting that injecting small malicious pieces of text into RAG Knowledge base can manipulate RAG behaviour with an ASR (Attack Success Rate) of 90\% across different benchmarks. Further study by \citet{eiras2025know} showed that commonly used LLM Judges are sensitive to distribution patterns and adversarial modifications in output. TrustRAG \citep{zhou2025trustrag} and RAGuard \citep{cheng2025raguard} proposed defence strategies, but they focused on generation and retrieval quality rather than evaluation reliability. Altogether, research and these findings point to a vulnerability in the system that questions the trustworthiness of LLM Judges. Whether the Judge itself is compromised by the corpus poisoning and whether upstream filtering can restore it has not been studied systematically.

\subsection{The Scale of the Evaluation Challenge}

To appreciate the urgency of this problem, it is instructive to consider the scale at which RAG evaluation is currently conducted. A typical enterprise RAG deployment may process thousands of user queries per day, each requiring retrieval from a knowledge base containing millions of documents, generation of a contextualised response, and evaluation of that response for quality assurance. Manual evaluation of even a small fraction of these interactions would require dedicated teams of subject-matter experts working full-time, at costs that dwarf the infrastructure expenses of the RAG system itself. The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm was adopted precisely because it offered a path to continuous, automated evaluation at this scale, but the security implications of this automation have not kept pace with its deployment.

The consequences of evaluation failure in this context are not merely academic. In financial services, where RAG systems are increasingly used for regulatory compliance queries and investment research synthesis, a judge that validates factually incorrect responses could expose organisations to regulatory sanctions, financial losses, or liability. In healthcare applications, where RAG systems assist clinicians with diagnostic support and treatment protocol queries, misleading evaluations could lead to the deployment of systems that provide medically dangerous advice while appearing to meet quality standards. In legal applications, inaccurate evaluation could result in the dissemination of incorrect legal guidance that undermines access to justice.

The dual nature of the vulnerability---affecting both the generation and evaluation layers---means that standard quality assurance processes are insufficient. An organisation that relies on automated evaluation to monitor its RAG system's performance would receive consistently positive signals even as the underlying system degrades due to context corruption. This creates a dangerous blind spot where the evaluation mechanism, rather than serving as a safety net, becomes part of the failure cascade. Adversaries, in turn, gain an additional incentive to poison retrieval corpora precisely because doing so suppresses quality signals while the compromised system continues to appear healthy.

\subsection{Origins of the Research}

The research questions investigated in this thesis emerged from the author's work with the AI Tech team at BNP Paribas on a production RAG evaluation pipeline for compliance use cases. The pipeline relied on LLM-as-a-Judge scoring along the standard RAGAS dimensions at a scale that made manual evaluation infeasible, and exposed two structural concerns: in regulated domains, the cost of an undetected evaluation failure is asymmetrically large, since a judge that validates an incorrect response grounded in altered policy documents propagates false assurance through downstream audit trails; and the judge itself has no principled way to verify the integrity of its inputs, since it can only assess whether the response is consistent with the retrieved context. These observations suggested a latent vulnerability in the paradigm that had not been systematically characterised in the academic literature, and that practical mitigations accessible to resource-constrained teams were underexplored. The present thesis provides both the characterisation and an empirical test of one mitigation class.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis targets the following fundamental problem: The conventional LLM judges applied within a RAG evaluation pipeline may not maintain its robustness against corpus poisoning attacks, and the efficacy of lightweight upstream filtering against corpus poisoning remains largely unexplored.

More specifically, in a RAG evaluation pipeline, an LLM judge is employed to score generated responses conditioned on the retrieved context. During evaluation, the judge ascertains whether the generated answer is factually supported by the retrieved context. However, under adversarial settings, the retrieved context itself might be poisoned or contains misleading information. In this situation, the generated response can still be deemed to be supported by the retrieved context even if the content in the retrieved evidence is false or maliciously created. Since the LLM judge does not have access to an outside source of ground truth, it can only evaluate the answer based on the provided context, which may be poisoned.

One simple and straightforward mitigation strategy is to apply filters on the retrieved contexts before they reach the judge. Various filter mechanisms have been proposed and studied, such as statistical anomaly detection, embedding-based filter, adversarial classifiers, and LLM-based moderation. However, it is not clear whether those filters will indeed enhance downstream judge robustness under realistic corpus poisoning situations where the attack is semantically coherent and retriever-aligned.

The problem is also further complicated by the fact that many existing defense mechanisms for RAG security are too costly and complicated to be employed in practice. Fine-tuning dedicated safety models, training adversarial retrievers, or deploying ensemble-based systems often require large computational resources, dedicated data sets, and significant engineering effort, all of which might not be accessible to individual researchers, small teams or low-resource scenarios. As a result, there is an immediate need for lightweight mitigation strategies and systematic empirical study of their performance and limitations under adversarial conditions.

\section{Research Questions}

This thesis is organised around four interconnected research questions that guide the investigation from diagnosis through mitigation design to failure analysis:

\textbf{Research Question 1 (RQ1) --- Baseline Vulnerability:} To what extent do current LLM judges (GPT-5.4-nano, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-IT, DeepSeek-V3.2) assign high faithfulness scores to responses generated from poisoned RAG contexts, and how does vulnerability vary across injection types and noise levels?

\textbf{Research Question 2 (RQ2) --- Comparative Judge Behaviour:} How do the three judges differ in their vulnerability profiles, score distributions, and calibration characteristics under corpus poisoning, and what do these differences reveal about the architectural and training factors that drive judge robustness?

\textbf{Research Question 3 (RQ3) --- Pre-filter Efficacy:} Can lightweight upstream pre-filter pipelines (statistical multi-signal or LLM-based) improve downstream judge reliability without substantially degrading clean-context performance?

\textbf{Research Question 4 (RQ4) --- Limits of Filtering:} How do different filtering strategies alter the residual context presented to downstream judges, and what limitations or trade-offs emerge from these interventions?

\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes four principal contributions to the fields of LLM evaluation, RAG security, and automated quality assessment.

\textbf{First, the Poisoned Evaluation Dataset (v2).} We construct and release a poisoned evaluation benchmark derived from HotpotQA, spanning multiple injection strategies and noise levels with explicit ground-truth poisoned passage annotations. The dataset incorporates a corrected PoisonedRAG-style injection procedure that separates target wrong-answer generation from malicious passage synthesis while preserving contextual coherence. The benchmark is designed specifically for studying evaluator robustness independently from generator behaviour.

\textbf{Second, a systematic meta-evaluation framework for contemporary LLM judges.} We develop an evaluation framework for analysing the robustness of three contemporary LLM judges across standard RAGAS evaluation dimensions under controlled corpus-poisoning conditions. The framework enables comparative analysis of judge calibration behaviour, vulnerability profiles, and scoring characteristics across multiple attack families.

\textbf{Third, a lightweight multi-signal pre-filtering pipeline.} We design a statistical passage-level pre-filter that combines embedding-based, probabilistic, discriminative, and relevance-based signals through an XGBoost aggregation layer. We additionally evaluate an LLM-based filtering strategy and compare both approaches in terms of classification behaviour and downstream evaluation impact.

\textbf{Fourth, a residual-aware analysis framework for filtered evaluation pipelines.} We introduce an analytical decomposition that characterises post-filter residual contexts according to support preservation and poison survival properties. This framework enables a more detailed investigation of how filtering interventions interact with downstream judge behaviour and provides insight into the limitations of current mitigation strategies.

Together with a comparative discussion of existing RAG defence approaches, these contributions advance the study of reliable automated evaluation under adversarial retrieval conditions.
