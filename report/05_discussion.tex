\chapter{Discussion}

The findings in Chapter~4 do not point to a single simple explanation. Some results did match what we expected from a poisoned RAG evaluation scenario, but others were more surprising. In the end, they raise a practical question: when the retrieved context itself may be poisoned, how much trust should we place in an LLM judge to catch it?
In this chapter, we discuss the main findings and what they mean in a real sense. We first take a look at the filtering paradox and explain our understanding behind it. We then compare the three judges, because their behaviour is clearly not interchangeable. After that, we relate the results to existing RAG defence methods, discuss possible mitigation strategies, outline the main limitations of the study, and close with practical takeaways for teams building LLM-as-a-Judge pipelines.


\section{The Filtering Paradox: Mechanism and Interpretation}

It's simple to state the main result of Chapter~4, but difficult to tell the why and how behind it. Statistical pre-filtering cannot guarantee protection of judges from corpus poisoning. In fact, under the most extreme adversarial circumstances it could move judges in the opposite direction than we wanted. This was not the outcome we anticipated when we set out to run this study.
The most extreme example we found was DeepSeek on the Survived portion with the PoisonedRAG style triplets. With the statistical filter, mean faithfulness increases from a baseline of 0.361 to 0.738, an increase of 0.377 (question-clustered bootstrap p-value of 0.012). This was an unexpected yet significant trend in this portion of the study and inspired the analysis in this thesis.
While the trends on the True-subset also move in the same direction, the effects are smaller and much less consistent. With the original supporting texts replaced, DeepSeek showed the starkest increase, whereas GPT and Gemma had smaller increases which could not maintain significance after more robust testing. Therefore, the trends are present in all three models, yet without a strong enough signal to pinpoint one singular failure mode.
Intuitively this seems quite strange. Statistical pre-filtering itself is a very powerful classifier, achieving a passage classification F1 score of 0.929. We would naturally expect that after eliminating poisoned, flagged passages, that the tasks themselves would be easier to judge and lead to more confident judgements based on clearer contexts. That's the simple story.
But the experiments do not support that simple story. Section~4.5 tested two possible explanations and pointed toward a third. We discuss these explanations below.


\subsection{Collateral damage is real but it is not the operative mechanism}

The first plausible reason for the paradox we consider is collateral damage. Collateral damage means that the filter removes the supporting evidence that supported the ground truth answer. This certainly does occur. Over the entire data set, some 17.5\% of the supporting passages were removed.
On first inspection, this appeared to be a credible account of the paradox. If the filter removes evidence that a judge requires, the remaining poisoned context may then be uncontradicted. The judge is then able to award a higher faithfulness score since the contradictory evidence is no longer apparent.
We investigate this hypothesis directly in Section~4.5.2. Taking the triplets from the True-subset, we compare the faithfulness scores from those that contain no collateral damage (the supporting passages survive filtering) and those that do. If collateral damage is the source of the paradox we should see the greatest increase in faithfulness score for those that contained collateral damage.
As it turns out, the reverse is true across all three judges; we see that in the case of collateral damage, the faithfulness score is lower by an average of between 0.15 and 0.23. This tells us that judges do, in fact, recognize the absence of key supporting evidence, and their faithfulness confidence decreases rather than increases.
Thus, although collateral damage does occur, it fails to account for the paradox and indeed seems to counter it: the paradox occurs most notably when the collateral damage doesn't take place---in other words when the supporting passages are not filtered. There is therefore another reason for the judges awarding the filtered context a higher degree of faithfulness.
The True-subset average in Chapter~4 is a resultant of two forces: when collateral damage occurs, which is in 27\% of cases, the score decreases, while when it does not occur (in 73\% of cases), the score tends to increase. In some circumstances, the second effect outweighs the first and generates the paradox.


\subsection{The operative mechanism is context clarification through distractor removal}

The better explanation is the distractor removal.
The statistical filter is designed to classify poison but incidentally removes many of the original HotpotQA distractor passages. The distractors were added into the data to increase difficulty of the multi-hop task, are often noisier, less relevant to the question, and further away from the question's embedding. They also bear similarities to random-noise injections, so the filter learned to remove a large proportion of them along with poisoned passages.
This alters the context reaching the judge. With filtering, the context becomes shorter: average length reduced by 39\% from 11.6 passages to 7.1 passages, and becomes more coherent as off-topic passages are removed first. The remaining poisoned passages become embedded in a cleaner, more relevant set of context and hence appear to be more trustworthy to the judge: the judge gives them higher faithfulness scores, even if the remaining context does not truly justify the answer.
This effect can be observed directly from Table~\ref{tab:distractor-removal-stratified}. If we group True-subset triplets by number of original distractors the filter removed, we can see that for all 3 judges, the paradox increased from almost none in the bottom quartile to $+0.25$--$+0.46$ in the top quartile. This is not just an anomaly.
Similarly, let's examine context length. The correlation between faithfulness and length at baseline is weak: GPT~$= 0.10$, Gemma~$= 0.29$, DeepSeek~$= -0.08$. Post-filter length correlation with faithfulness is stronger: GPT~$= 0.36$, Gemma~$= 0.47$, DeepSeek~$= 0.31$. This demonstrates that shorter post-filter contexts receive higher faithfulness scores than longer contexts, which wasn't significantly true before filtering.


\subsection{The LLM filter does the same thing. Just less of it.}

Mistral sees the full triplet: the question, the context, and the answer, and flags individual passages as suspicious. Those passages are removed before the judge scores. Same pipeline structure as the statistical filter, same removal mechanism. The difference is how aggressively it prunes: on average 1.2 passages per triplet, compared to 4.5 for the statistical filter.

That lighter touch still produces the paradox, just more quietly. The context gets slightly shorter. Slightly more coherent. Judges still read that cleaner residual as more trustworthy. For DeepSeek, the True-subset mean rises by 0.053 under the LLM filter --- smaller than the statistical filter's 0.151, but the direction is the same. On the Survived subset the effect remains robust well past the 0.05 threshold (corrected $p < 0.001$ for DeepSeek).

There is one additional cost the statistical filter does not carry. The LLM filter's clean false-positive rate is 0.174, roughly one in six legitimate triplets has at least one passage wrongly removed even when there is nothing adversarial going on. The statistical filter's equivalent is 0.012. That gap matters in practice. The more aggressively you deploy the LLM filter, the more you quietly damage the evaluations you actually wanted to keep.


\subsection{Why this is a paradox in the practical sense}

We continue to call it a paradox, as the filtering process leads to two opposing effects. On a local level, the filter achieves its purpose; it takes out what seems like a poisoned passage. On a global level, the judge can develop increased confidence in the context that remains, even though that context may still be false.
The standard filter-then-evaluate intuition is straight forward. The poison contaminates the context, the filter removes the poison, the remaining context is cleaner and the judge receives a cleaner signal. This makes sense.
What we demonstrate is that the residual is indeed cleaner and that is precisely what is wrong. Cleaner residuals make for a more trustworthy context from the perspective of the judge. A short, relevant, well-formed looking context will seem credible, but credible does not mean evidenced; the judge may not be separating the two well enough.
This suggests that the engineering task is different from what we initially assume. Most filters are evaluated using passage level metrics such as precision, recall, and F1. These will only inform us of whether the filter successfully identifies poison; they won't tell us what impact the filtered residual has on the downstream judge. We've shown these goals pull in opposite directions: an aggressive filter might lead to a context more likely to be trusted by the judge, including one in which some poison remains.


\subsection{Implications for filter design}

Three points follow from this.
First, passage-level classifier F1 isn't the right criterion on which to design filters. For LLM-as-a-Judge pipelines, the relevant metric is end-to-end judge behaviour on a held-out set with authentic poison rates. It is possible to design a good filter classifier and have the filter degrade the reliability of the judge.
Second, removal of poison is not identical to removal of distractors. If a filter aggressively removes out-of-domain content, it might catch some poison, but it removes the native retrieval noise that provided the original skepticism signal for the judge. This leads to the hypothesis that some other methods of filter implementation like re-ranking, contradiction detection or warning-based filters may be safer than simple deletion.
Third, the observed dependency on context length implies that judges use length as a reliability signal. This isn't inherently problematic. Under non-adversarial conditions, the shorter and more focused the retrieved context, the more likely it is to be informative, and the more likely it is that a judgment on the retrieved document will be accurate. The problem, then, is that this thumb-rule will be susceptible to exploitation if the shorter context comes from a filter rather than honest retrieval. The model could be made aware of the fact that the retrieved document has been filtered, enabling it to adjust its judging. While Section~4.6 provides a minimal signal in this direction qualitatively, more rigorous tests of this idea need to be performed.

\section{What the Cross-Judge Differences Mean}
\label{sec:judge-calibration}

The 3 judges used in the study were all modern LLMs but occupied different places in the model landscape. There were proprietary, open-weight and low-cost judges, each having different training data and architecture but all being strong performing models. Their performance differences in our experiment are due to scoring behaviour and not raw capability. GPT models continuously output scores spanning across the $[0,1]$ range as the strength of evidence changes. Gemma responds differently, giving a high faithfulness score by default unless the evidence against it being faithful is strong. DeepSeek's behaviour is different again; nearly binary and scoring almost all the way at one extreme or the other, its score represents less of a gradual judgement than more of a verdict. It either says yes or no.
All of these scoring strategies have their pros and cons. GPT models' continuous scoring allows the full $[0,1]$ range to act as a signal downstream, whether that's for filtering, ranking, or human review of low-confidence results. Gemma's tendency towards giving high scores makes it less useful since a high score can be considered the default. The low scores Gemma does provide are more useful, but only sparingly. DeepSeek works in the opposite direction, yielding very definite results, though this might be more definite than a human would consider a judgment to be. High baseline FPR for DeepSeek seems to point to this fact.
One of the most surprising results in Chapter~4 was the cross-judge reversal of the PoisonedRAG-style attacks. GPT and Gemma perform the worst on this category, while DeepSeek handles it better. This indicates that the complexity of an attack does not translate into being an attack that every judge finds hard. An attack that may appear coherent, topical, and aligned on retriever performance, may be difficult for some judges and easy for others, depending on the judge's particular scoring algorithm. DeepSeek seems to leverage its binary scoring algorithm as some sort of contradiction-detection mechanism. What the poisoned paragraph claims conflicts with the ground-truth answer. Once the contradiction has been discovered, DeepSeek seems to ``set it'' and score it low. The gradually scoring models (GPT and Gemma) seem to trade off the contradiction against other factors in the paragraph like topical relevance, fluency, etc.

We have two practical takeaways.
First, the choice of judge is a security issue. We found that many LLM-as-a-Judge pipelines are selecting judges based on price or availability, treating two judges at the same capability level as interchangeable. This is risky. For the three judges and one task that we examined, robustness against corpus poisoning varied dramatically across judges. And it was the judge with the highest accuracy on unpoisoned text that performed worst against poison. Of course we do not know if this holds generally for all models or all tasks. That would require many more judges and more datasets. But the pattern is strong enough that it is likely teams ought to measure baseline robustness under likely adversarial circumstances before deciding which judge to use in a product pipeline.
Second, averaging across judges can hide interesting phenomena. If we had just presented the mean baseline FPR for all three judges, we would not have found the directional reversal. Multi-judge studies should report results for each judge separately, rather than aggregated data. Different calibration mechanisms respond to attacks differently.
We do not claim that any of the regimes presented here is always the best. All have pros and cons. There is no one-size-fits-all, and the optimum choice depends on the specific threat model and how the rest of the pipeline uses judge output. The essential message of this study is clear: we must measure judge calibration against adversarial attack and build downstream systems accordingly, prior to product use.


\section{Comparison with Existing RAG Defences}

There are already many defence mechanisms presented in RAG security literature. The overwhelming majority attempt to protect the generator side of the system. This thesis focuses on a different part of the pipeline: the judge.
Detection-based defence mechanisms, such as perplexity checks and embedding clustering, rely on assumptions about unusual statistical or semantic properties of adversarial text. PoisonedRAG \citep{zou2025poisonedrag} found that clean and malicious passages are heavily overlapped in terms of perplexity, and PR-Attack \citep{jiao2025prattack} complicates this by minimising perplexity. Perplexity-based defences become ineffective in this scenario.
Similar limitations apply to embedding clustering. TrustRAG \citep{zhou2025trustrag}, for example, assumes that the malicious documents form an isolated cluster in embedding space. This assumption is weakened when the attacker is able to retrieve a large number of malicious documents, though it may be effective when a few poisoning points have been injected. Our statistical filter relies on similar signals, so it suffers from similar issues when viewed in isolation, but it combines these signals with others.
Aggregation-based defence mechanisms, such as RobustRAG \citep{xiang2024robustrag}, combine the generation outputs from many separate generation invocations over individual passages through some form of voting. These attacks only fail when the malicious documents constitute a minority. However, the main issue is that aggregation works on the generator side and is a proxy for the correctness of the judges.
Adversarial training of the retriever/generator leads to robustness of the generator but requires representative attack data that matches future attacks and does not apply directly to the judge.
Process-based defence mechanisms, such as InstructRAG \citep{wei2025instructrag}, AstuteRAG \citep{wang2025astuterag}, and Discern-and-Answer \citep{hong2024gullible}, can sometimes assist against denial-of-service style attacks but may be less well suited to targeted poisoning.
RAGDefender \citep{kim2025ragdefender} is closest to this thesis: a lightweight ML filter situated between retrieval and downstream consumers. It aims to measure Attack Success Rate and protect the generator. This thesis places a similar filter to protect the judge, measure FPR along the 3 dimensions of RAGAS \citep{es2024ragas}, and compares the statistical filter with an LLM-based one, decomposed into its true and survived effects to analyse judge reliability.
The most important lesson from this thesis is that defence mechanisms aimed at the generator do not automatically protect the judge; the same filter can perform differently based on the downstream target.


\section{Mitigation Strategies the Results Point Toward}

From this, there are several approaches towards mitigating these problems. None of these can be considered as an end solution but provide potential steps in this direction:

\textbf{Hybrid filter architectures.} The statistical and LLM filters have nearly-disjoint success on the different forms of poison. The statistical filter has stronger end-to-end recall on replacement-based attacks (random noise 0.554, adversarial fact 0.551) and lower recall on PoisonedRAG-style (0.296). The LLM filter has the opposite profile: stronger on PoisonedRAG-style (0.765) and adversarial fact (0.610), but weak on random noise (0.140). A hybrid filter might use the statistical filter on replacement-style poison and send suspicious triplets to the LLM filter. This could yield better classification accuracy. It might not yield better judge reliability, however. It is likely the answer is no, at least at present (see Section~5.1), as the hybrid filter, with its higher recall, also likely removes many more distractors and yields shorter, tidier remaining contexts. That may just bring back the paradox in more severe form.

\textbf{Calibration-aware judge selection and ensembling.} We established in Section~\ref{sec:judge-calibration} that the three judges have varied calibration regimes and are responsive to attack type to differing extents. A practitioner could select a judge on the basis of the threat model: they might use a judge with the characteristics of DeepSeek if facing a PoisonedRAG-style attack, and one with confidence-abstention such as GPT if facing a noise/edit style attack. A pipeline approach could use various calibration regimes: in contrast to the detection mechanisms from Section~4.7 which relied on variance, this would increase robustness against different failure modes at the expense of cost (3 judges per triplet rather than 1).

\textbf{Confidence abstention for continuous-scoring judges.} This constant calibrating of GPT creates an inherent band where decisions will naturally be avoided. A pipeline might not make a decision for triplets that fall within $[0.4,0.7]$ in faithfulness scores and pass those through for human evaluation. Based on the distributions of scores presented in Section~4.1.2 it is likely that an abstention rate between 10 and 15\% would cover majority of the remaining judge error after filtering.
This introduces an increased workload on humans for review. However, this review workload is placed in areas where judge error is concentrated. This strategy was not tested in this thesis but remains a viable experiment for future work.

\textbf{Judge-side hardening.} If pre-filtering does not produce residuals that the judge is likely to treat well, the judge needs to be hardened. Several approaches may be taken. Judges can be fine-tuned on adversarial examples; the judge's scoring prompt can be changed such that it must check for cross-passage consistency before scoring; or an additional meta-evaluation layer can score how trustworthy the context is.
The justification analysis (Section~4.6) indicates that explicit poison-aware prompting may move judge responses toward more normal patterns on PoisonedRAG-style attacks. This is the direction about which our data are most conclusive among the described mitigation approaches. A pragmatic consideration also comes from this approach; it can be applied without modifying the filter itself.
These approaches can be applied in conjunction with each other. A pipeline could use a statistical filter initially, redirect indeterminate samples to an LLM filter, abstain on uncertain scores, and then use a poison-aware prompt for the judge. The question of how well the individual strategies combined are beyond the scope of this analysis.


\section{Limitations}

The limitations of this work are as follows:

\textbf{Single-domain evaluation.} The experiments presented here were all performed using HotpotQA. As such, it should not be interpreted that these results apply to all LLM-as-a-Judge systems. All attacks used in this thesis were constructed in the multi-passage retrieval paradigm and exploit properties of it (e.g., answer support being spread over multiple passages). Furthermore, signals used to construct the pre-filter (e.g. Cosine anomaly, answer-span recall, cross-encoder relevance) are all tuned to properties of this setting.

It remains an open question whether the central paradox holds true in single-hop QA, dialog evaluation, summarization faithfulness, or open-domain agentic evaluation. Any extrapolation beyond other multi-passage retrieval tasks seems overly speculative. This constraint to a single task is arguably the most significant limitation of this thesis.

\textbf{Three judges, not a representative panel.} Our sample of three LLM judges reflects three distinct kinds of contemporary model: one closed-source proprietary model, one open-weight foundation model, and one cost-effective service. Given only $n = 3$ judges, we cannot generalize about LLM judges. However, we demonstrate here that judges across these regimes may still share a core set of behaviours and vulnerabilities, differing only in terms of calibration, reaction to advanced attack types, and their sensitivity to the True-subset paradox with filtering.

The addition of a fourth or fifth judge (whether reasoning models, frontier models or fine-tuned models) may support, contradict, or challenge our observations. Thus, interpretations like ``model architecture can matter as much as model size'' are based on trends identified through this admittedly small panel and are not yet fully confirmed. It would be straightforward to test such an expansion and is perhaps the clearest immediate direction for research beyond this thesis.

\textbf{No human-annotated ground truth.} This work measures the consistency between LLM judges with respect to construction-time poison labels on the triplets. We do not compare judge-generated outputs to human annotations of faithfulness for the same triplet. Instead, we inherit from RAGAS the assumption that LLM judges can serve as effective proxies for human judgment.

In consequence, all our observations about what occurs ``under attack'' must be interpreted as relative shifts in how our specific judges behave, not as definitively ``correct'' measures of faithfulness under poison. Measuring performance on human-annotated test data (even a small, hand-picked set of 50 triplets across three annotators) would enable stronger statements in future research.

\textbf{Question-level clustering in significance tests.} The 1,200 poisoned triplets used in this study were constructed from 100 different questions. Each question has 12 triplets: three injection types and four noise levels, each with the same ground-truth question and answer. Thus, these triplets are not independent.

We addressed this by using a question-clustered bootstrap procedure with 1,000 iterations on the McNemar tests for Section~4.4.5. We provide the bootstrap p-values alongside the uncorrected ones in Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar-true-faithfulness}. Although directional effects persist in many of these, we note that our True-subset significant claims are weakened when using bootstrap corrections (bootstrap p-value = 0.239 for GPT, 0.259 for Gemma; $p = 0.099$ for DeepSeek). The only effect that remains reliably significant with this bootstrap is the DeepSeek Survived-subset shift on PoisonedRAG-style triplets (bootstrap $p = 0.012$). We therefore defer to the bootstrap values for all significance claims made based on this test.

\textbf{Filter design choices.} The statistical filter we developed consists of five handcrafted signals, combined with an XGBoost model. The signals were chosen specifically because they could be expected to react to the types of poison implemented here. Other choices of signals, including learned feature representations or a sequence-labelling approach to passage relevance, could potentially result in different outcomes. The Mistral filter also uses a single-sentence prompt per triplet; a different prompt configuration could alter its behaviour.

\textbf{Single dataset version.} All evaluations reported here were conducted on a single version of our Poisoned Evaluation Dataset (100 questions, 2,400 total triplets). Although this dataset is sufficient to observe the general trends demonstrated in this work, some of the subsets of the data used for the analysis have a smaller number of samples. For instance, the True-subset has 576 total triplets; this becomes smaller still when split by judge, condition, and injection type. More samples would likely decrease the confidence intervals for these statistics, but would likely not change the direction of observed effects.

\textbf{The True/Survived split is asymmetric across injection types.} For replacement-based attacks where one or more supporting passages are altered (or deleted) and replaced by another, this method of partitioning the data into True/Survived components works cleanly; all poison-bearing passages belong to a class where the judge should ideally not find evidence from the original passages. In contrast, for additive attacks (i.e. PoisonedRAG-style), all triplets by definition have no passages replaced; all fall into the Survived subset.

While this is theoretically appropriate (the added poison does not invalidate the original supporting passages) it makes the True/Survived dichotomy less useful when examining additive attacks. Other ways of decomposing cases exist, e.g., by whether the judge can still successfully utilize a majority of evidence documents post-filter. Our structural approach is easier to compute from poison labels, but is not the only possible choice.

\textbf{Production deployability of the True/Survived analysis.} Our definition of the True/Survived splits requires knowing the characteristics of the poison (which passage was overwritten, if any). This is only possible within the context of a dataset we control. It is impossible to directly compute the True-subset FPR on a model deployed with an unspecified poison or in an adversarial context without these labels.

What is achievable in production with a filter and our approach is to measure how judge outputs change when filtered over a representative poison set. The method described here only enables analysis of filter performance in offline evaluation scenarios such as those performed in this thesis. Offline evaluation over benchmark poisoned datasets is thus the most direct application.

\textbf{Justification analysis sample size.} All analysis of judge justifications was performed on only 28 individual triplets (scored by a single judge under three prompt types). It serves as an illustration and supplemental data to our quantitative findings, but it is not sufficient for strong quantitative claims.

\textbf{Mechanism evidence is empirical but bounded.} We have provided three key pieces of evidence supporting the primary mechanism---context clarification through distractor removal: counterfactual analysis against collateral damage, stratifying performance based on number of removed distractors, and length-based correlations showing the benefit of shorter post-filter contexts. These tests all suggest the removal of distractors helps the judges focus and maintain faithfulness, while also effectively ruling out the most likely alternative explanations.

These results, however, are still empirical and provide suggestive, but not definitively causal, support for this hypothesis. We cannot claim a perfect, guaranteed understanding of the judges' mechanisms. The evidence should be seen as most strongly supporting the hypothesis in light of elimination of alternatives, rather than through definitive isolation. For example, it would be ideal to further control for context length independently of the poison content.


\section{Practical Implications}

This work suggests the following practical guidelines to teams designing LLM-as-a-Judge RAG pipelines.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Baseline poisoned judge FPRs are too high for security-critical applications. In our work, we found the baseline faithfulness FPRs to be between 41\%--70\% for the 3 judges we examined. This makes it unacceptable for critical compliance monitoring, financial or medical reviews, and legal analysis. Teams should assume that an unfiltered single judge pipeline is not a final solution for these scenarios.
\item Light-weight upstream filtering does not address the issue. An intuitive idea is to prepend the judge with a filter. The results here, however, suggest that filters may transform the residual context into one in which the judge actually has higher confidence in poisoned data. Simply prepending a filter, and assuming reliability has increased, may thus not be safe.
\item The choice of judge is a security decision. Since our 3 judges react quite differently to the more advanced attack class, pipeline designers must take care when choosing judges and cannot simply select them based on price. Judges should be tested under an assumption of a threat model, before a pipeline is designed for a production setting. The Poisoned Evaluation Dataset accompanying this thesis can help achieve this.
\item Multi-judge ensembling is not a replacement for filtering. A pipeline designer cannot expect to achieve high detection rates simply through averaging signals from judges' disagreement. Their disagreement signal is simply not large enough to support threshold-based decisions with any meaningful certainty. Multi-judge pipelines are, of course, still useful for other things (API failures, average calibration, redundancy) but not poisoning detection.
\item Judge-side hardening looks more promising than simply trying to optimise the filter. From the justification analysis, explicit poisoning-aware prompting could shift judge behaviour for PoisonedRAG style attacks. This method is a pragmatic approach because it does not require any work on filters. Its effectiveness on real-world tasks remains to be seen, but early results appear promising.
\item Evaluation methodology must be carefully considered. When evaluating filters, teams cannot simply use classifier F1. An upstream filter may have perfect F1 on a separate, random pass of the corpus but its output may be handled worse by downstream judges than the unfiltered corpus. Ultimately, the primary concern is end-to-end judge reliability.
\end{enumerate}
