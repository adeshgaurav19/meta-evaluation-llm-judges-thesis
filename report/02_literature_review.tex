\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Its Evaluation}

\subsection{The RAG Paradigm}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation addresses two well-known limitations of standalone LLMs: hallucinations, and the inability to access knowledge beyond what was in the training data. The idea was first put forward by \citet{lewis2020retrieval}, who paired a dense retriever with a generative model so that the model's outputs could be grounded in external chunks of data fetched at inference time. The main plus of RAG is that knowledge storage becomes separate from language understanding, and can be updated without retraining the model.

The idea has since grown in several directions. \citet{karpukhin2020dense} introduced dense passage retrieval, which trains encoders for queries and passages so that semantically similar content ends up close in embedding space. Later work added re-ranking stages, hybrid sparse-dense retrieval, and iterative retrieval-generation loops. A modern RAG pipeline typically has four stages: query understanding, retrieval, re-ranking, and generation.

In short, RAG came from a simple realisation. A model does not need to memorise facts if it can look them up at inference time.

\subsection{The RAGAS Evaluation Framework}

RAG evaluation is hard for reasons that traditional NLP metrics do not address. BLEU and ROUGE were designed for tasks like summarisation, where you assume a small set of correct outputs and score against them. RAG outputs are open-ended. A single query can have many correct and valid responses. Human evaluation is the obvious fallback, but it is too expensive at the production scale and too slow as well.

\citet{es2024ragas} proposed RAGAS, which showed that automated evaluation using LLM judges is feasible across three metrics. Faithfulness checks whether the response is grounded in the context. Answer relevance and context relevance check whether the answer relates to the query, and whether the retrieved context actually addresses the question. Each metric is scored on a continuous scale. RAGAS is now the default in both academic and industry RAG evaluation.

The key idea is straightforward. If you need a model to evaluate answers, maybe another model can do it, which saves the cost of human annotators while keeping the evaluation meaningful at scale.

\subsection{Known Limitations of LLM Judges}

LLM-as-a-Judge has several documented failure modes. \citet{wang2023large} showed that in pairwise comparisons, judges prefer responses based on the order they appear in (position bias). \citet{saito2023verbosity} found that judges tend to favour longer responses irrespective of content (verbosity bias). And \citet{panickssery2024self} reported that judges favour responses from their own model family (self-enhancement bias). Taken together, these biases say something uncomfortable: LLM judges are not model-agnostic evaluators. Changing the surface features of the input can shift the score independently of quality.

Calibration is a separate problem. Some judges produce continuous scores on $[0, 1]$, others collapse toward binary outputs, and \citet{zheng2023judging} pointed out that judges from different model families differ systematically in how they assign scores at all. That makes cross-judge comparisons and ensemble methods harder than they look.

What is striking across these papers is that judge scores often respond to things unrelated to answer quality. Where a response sits, how long it is, whether it sounds like the same model family. None of these should matter, but they do.

\subsection{LLM-as-a-Judge Studies on Contemporary Model Families}

The three judges we use in this thesis (GPT-5.4-nano, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-IT, and DeepSeek-V3.2) were all released between Q1 2025 and Q4 2025. Each has been examined in some adjacent role, but none has been tested under a corpus-poisoning setting. The relevant prior work is summarised below.

The closest precedent for nano-class GPT-5 judges is \citet{antaki2025gpt5}. They benchmark twelve configurations of the GPT-5 series, including the nano variants, on a medical-question-answering task. Using a reference-anchored pairwise LLM-as-a-Judge setup, they report that GPT-5-high outperforms the GPT-5-nano variants on both accuracy and rationale quality. Their work also doubles as a useful reference for the verbosity and position bias mitigations needed when deploying nano-class judges in high-stakes domains.

\citet{lail2026empirical} looks at practical LLM-as-a-Judge improvement techniques on RewardBench 2 across GPT and Claude model families. The study tests task-specific criteria injection, ensemble scoring, calibration context, and adaptive escalation, and finds that ensemble scoring combined with criteria injection reaches 85.8 percent accuracy, a 13.5 percentage-point improvement over baseline. It also treats per-response score variance as an uncertainty signal for judge error. The setting is standard reward modelling, not an adversarial one.

For Gemma-4-26B-A4B-IT, we did not find published work that characterises it in an absolute-scoring judge role. Gemma 4 was Google's open-weight release at the time of our experiment design and attracted substantial attention as an open alternative to proprietary models in the same size class. We picked it as the open-weight judge in our panel partly for that reason. It represents the kind of model practitioners reach for when cost or data-residency constraints rule out closed APIs. The fact that its behaviour as a judge in RAG evaluation has not been documented anywhere we could find was an additional reason to study it.

DeepSeek's situation is similar. The family has been widely evaluated as a generation model on standard benchmarks, but we did not find work that places any DeepSeek model in a judge or judge-adjacent classifier role analogous to ours. Its behaviour as an evaluator, under either clean or adversarial conditions, is essentially uncharacterised in the LLM-as-a-Judge literature.

Beyond these judge-specific papers, \citet{li2024llms} provide a comprehensive overview of LLM-as-a-Judge techniques, benchmarks, and open problems, while \citet{li2025scoring} formally defines rubric order bias, score ID bias, and reference answer score bias, extending the bias taxonomy further than the position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases established by \citet{zheng2023judging}.

Put together, the existing work covers GPT-family judges in adjacent contexts (medical QA in \citet{antaki2025gpt5}, general reward modelling in \citet{lail2026empirical}) and leaves Gemma-4-26B-A4B-IT and DeepSeek-V3.2 essentially untouched as judges. None of the three has been evaluated under corpus poisoning, which is the gap this thesis sets out to fill.

\section{Corpus Poisoning Attacks on RAG Systems}

\subsection{The PoisonedRAG Framework}

PoisonedRAG \citep{zou2025poisonedrag} was the first systematic study of corpus poisoning against RAG. Its central observation is that a successful attack needs a malicious passage to do two things at once. It has to be ranked high enough to be retrieved for the target query, and once retrieved, it has to push the generator toward the attacker's chosen answer.

The construction PoisonedRAG uses to satisfy both conditions is a two-part passage. The first part is the target query itself, which boosts retrieval similarity through direct lexical and semantic overlap. The second part is an LLM-generated passage that supports the target incorrect answer in fluent, plausible-sounding prose. The combination is both retrievable and persuasive, and it achieves attack success rates above 90 percent across multiple benchmarks and generator models.

One finding from the paper matters more than the rest for our purposes. The black-box variant, which uses no access to retriever parameters, performs about as well as the white-box variant that assumes full gradient access. Retriever opacity does not buy much protection. An attacker who only knows the heuristic principles of semantic similarity can still build effective malicious passages.

What is uncomfortable about PoisonedRAG is the simplicity of the construction. Write a passage that looks relevant and says the wrong thing. A 90\% attack success rate follows almost automatically.

\subsection{Universal Adversarial Attacks on LLM Judges}

\citet{raina2024llm} showed that short adversarial phrases, when appended to candidate responses, can inflate the scores given by LLM judges regardless of how good the response actually is. The phrases come out of gradient-based optimisation against a set of target judges and exploit specific features the judge attends to. Because they generalise across queries and responses, the vulnerability is structural rather than instance-specific.

\citet{shi2024judgedeceiver} pushed this further with JudgeDeceiver, which builds universal templates that sway a judge model without access to its internal parameters. Black-box transferability is what makes this kind of attack practical against the evaluation systems deployed in industry, where the judge is usually behind an API.

Fooling a judge, in other words, does not require touching the retrieved context at all. A score shift can come purely from phrases appended to the answer.

\subsection{Distribution Shift and Stylistic Sensitivity}

\citet{eiras2025know} studied the meta-evaluation robustness of LLM safety judges and found that judges are surprisingly fragile to distribution shifts that humans would consider trivial. Small style changes (bullet points, a storytelling tone, similar surface features) produced large shifts in judge behaviour, with false negative rates rising by up to 0.24 on the same underlying dataset.

The implication is not limited to safety. If a judge is responsive to stylistic changes that have nothing to do with content quality, it will also respond to other kinds of context manipulation. RAG evaluation is one such case. Retrieved passages can carry patterns or text that confuse the judge, whether those were placed there adversarially or not. And if surface features as small as bullet points can shift scores, it is not a stretch to expect that context-level manipulation, which is far more substantial, will too.

\section{Defence Mechanisms for RAG Systems}

\subsection{Perplexity-Based Detection}

Perplexity is one of the simplest things you can compute on a passage, and the obvious first thing to try when filtering a corpus. The intuition is that adversarially crafted text, especially text produced by gradient-based optimisation, looks statistically weird and registers as high-perplexity against a clean reference distribution. Set a threshold, drop anything above it, and the malicious content is gone before it reaches the generator or the judge.

But this does not work reliably. \citet{zou2025poisonedrag} showed in the original PoisonedRAG paper that the perplexity distributions of clean and malicious passages overlap substantially. Choosing a threshold that catches a useful fraction of the poison also drops a lot of clean passages. The black-box variant is even more resistant, because its malicious text is built from two natural-looking pieces: the question itself, which is grammatical, and an LLM-generated passage, which is fluent. Neither piece looks anomalous on a perplexity scan. Later work like PR-Attack \citep{jiao2025prattack} uses bilevel optimisation to craft coordinated prompt-RAG attacks with enhanced stealth, closing off simple statistical defences even further. The lesson here is one of those general lessons about adversarial settings: any single-signal filter is an arms race the defender eventually loses. Once the attacker knows the signal, they optimise around it. This is the main reason most recent defence work has moved toward combinations of complementary signals.

\subsection{Embedding and Clustering Approaches}

TrustRAG \citep{zhou2025trustrag} comes at the problem differently. Its hypothesis is that malicious passages, having been optimised to be retrieved for the same target query, end up clustering together in embedding space. Run K-means on the retrieved set and the malicious cluster should separate from the clean retrieval distribution. After clustering, TrustRAG layers cosine similarity and ROUGE checks to confirm which passages in a suspicious cluster are likely poisoned, and then uses the LLM's own knowledge to resolve disagreements between external passages and the model's priors.

The assumption is elegant but fragile in practice. It works when the poison-to-clean ratio is high enough for the clusters to form distinctly. In more realistic settings, where poisoned documents are a minority of retrievals, comparative evaluations report that TrustRAG has a high false positive rate and flags many benign passages as poisoned. The same pattern recurs across the defence literature: aggressive detection lowers attack success rates but hurts retrieval quality on clean queries. Lightweight approaches that try to balance the two are what motivate the pre-filter studied here.

\subsection{RAGuard and Retrieval-Side Filtering}

RAGuard \citep{cheng2025raguard} is the closest existing work to what we build in this thesis, because it acts before poisoned text reaches the generator. It first expands the retrieval scope so the retrieved set contains more clean passages, then applies chunk-wise perplexity filtering and text-similarity filtering to flag suspicious texts. One useful property is that the defence is non-parametric, so it does not require training a new classifier for every attack variant. The remaining gap, and the one this thesis targets, is that RAGuard is designed mainly to protect the generator's final answer. Our question is whether an upstream filter of a similar kind can protect the evaluator itself, when the evaluator is an LLM judge.

\subsection{The CAP Method and Judge-Side Defences}

Comparative Augmented Prompting (CAP) works from a different observation. Comparative evaluation tasks (which response is better, A or B) hold up better against adversarial pressure than absolute scoring tasks (give this response a score from 0 to 1). CAP wraps a comparative step around the absolute scoring task to inherit some of that robustness. Tested on SummEval and TopicalChat, it lowers attack success rates without hurting clean evaluation quality much.

CAP is a judge-side defence: it modifies what the judge does, not what the judge sees. Instead of cleaning the input, you change how the judge reasons about it. That puts CAP on a different layer of the pipeline from the pre-filter studied in this thesis, which sits between the retriever and the judge and modifies the input.

\section{Multi-Signal Anomaly Detection and Classification}

\subsection{DeBERTa and Discriminative Classifiers}

DeBERTa \citep{he2021deberta} is BERT with two changes: disentangled attention, which separates content and position information, and an enhanced mask decoder. DeBERTa-v3 added replaced-token detection during pretraining, and the small variant keeps most of the classification performance with far fewer parameters. We picked DeBERTa-v3-small (140M parameters) for the classifier signal in our pre-filter for three practical reasons. It performs well on short-text classification, which is what we are doing. It trains in about 25 minutes on a free-tier Kaggle T4 GPU for our setup. The practical constraint of training within Kaggle GPU time limits mattered as much here as any theoretical performance gap between architectures. And it runs on CPU at inference time, which matters if you want to deploy the filter without specialised hardware.

We also trained a RoBERTa-base classifier \citep{liu2019roberta} as a comparison baseline. RoBERTa is a well-established model with a similar parameter count, and the comparison checks whether the choice of backbone matters much for the poisoned-passage classification task. Results are reported in the ablation analysis of Chapter 4.

\subsection{XGBoost for Feature Aggregation}

XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} is a gradient-boosted tree ensemble. It is the standard tool for combining heterogeneous tabular features into a single decision, and it is hard to find a Kaggle competition where it does not show up. In our pre-filter, XGBoost takes the five per-passage signal scores as input and outputs a poisoned-or-not probability. We picked it over a linear combination because the signals interact in non-linear ways. A passage with moderate cosine anomaly is suspicious only if the entropy signal also fires, and so on. Trees handle this kind of interaction naturally. A single mild anomaly is not suspicious on its own; two or three firing together usually are, and XGBoost picks that up without any hand-tuning.

\subsection{Cross-Encoders for Relevance}

Cross-encoders score a query-passage pair jointly. The model takes both as input in a single forward pass and produces one relevance score, attending to all token interactions across the pair. Bi-encoders, by contrast, encode the query and the passage independently and compare them with cosine similarity or a dot product. The cross-encoder is more accurate because it can model fine-grained interactions, but it is also slower because passage embeddings cannot be precomputed independently. That accuracy-speed trade-off is why cross-encoders are typically used for re-ranking a small candidate set after a fast bi-encoder retrieval, rather than as a first-pass retrieval tool. It is also what makes them fit naturally as a re-ranking signal in our pre-filter, applied only to the small retrieved set the judge eventually sees.

\section{Research Gap and Positioning of the Present Work}

What the literature in this chapter shows is a clear gap at the intersection of LLM-as-a-Judge robustness and corpus poisoning. RAG defences mostly target the generator or the retriever, with the goal of keeping malicious passages from influencing the final answer. The evaluator itself has received much less attention. Work on judge robustness, in turn, has concentrated on attacks that manipulate the judge directly through adversarial prompts, response formatting, or optimisation-based phrase injection, rather than attacks that influence evaluation indirectly by corrupting the retrieved context.

We did not find prior work that examines how contemporary LLM judges behave when evaluating responses grounded in poisoned retrieval contexts, nor how upstream filtering interventions change downstream judge reliability under those conditions. This thesis takes on that gap through a controlled empirical study across multiple judges, attack strategies, and filtering approaches. The methodology and the resulting analyses follow in the next chapters.
